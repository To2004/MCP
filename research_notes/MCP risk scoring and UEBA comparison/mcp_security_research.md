# MCP security literature beyond risk scoring (2024 – November 2026)

Scope: MCP-specific academic work that does not itself compute a risk score: surveys and SoKs, threat taxonomies, attack papers, benchmarks, ecosystem measurements, and defences (authorization, least privilege, sandboxing, provenance, signing, identity/OAuth, runtime monitoring, audit). Each entry has a **direction label**:

- **[A→S]**: the MCP server and the organisation's data behind it are protected; the agent (or its call) is the threat. This holds whether the agent misbehaves on its own (over-scoping, misuse), because a user steers it, or because injected content hijacks it. What counts is that the control or measurement constrains the agent's request at the server/tool boundary. This is our framework's direction.
- **[S→A]**: a malicious or compromised server, tool metadata, or tool output is the threat; the agent, its user, or its host is protected.
- **[Both]**: both directions, or neutral infrastructure (datasets, methods).

Method: candidates came from the repo's two earlier literature notes (`docs/project/annotated-bibliography-mcp-security.md`, `reports/experiments/dynamic_dimensions/knowledge_base/02-mcp-runtime-security-literature.md`), arXiv API title/abstract searches (2026-11-04, sorted by submission date), the Semantic Scholar/arXiv research tool, and Exa web search for peer-reviewed venues. **Every arXiv ID below was resolved against `export.arxiv.org/api` on 2026-11-04.** The title, first-version date and abstract were read from the API. Venue claims come from the arXiv comment/journal-ref field unless marked otherwise. Facts carried over from the repo's earlier note, and not re-read here, are marked "(repo note)". Dates are first arXiv submission dates.

## Which surveys and SoK papers on MCP security exist, and what taxonomies do they propose?

### Takeaway
At least 17 MCP-specific surveys, SoKs, taxonomies and threat models exist (Mar 2025 – Nov 2026). Almost all organise threats around the malicious server, tool metadata or supply chain. Only three name the *calling agent or user* as an adversary class: Hou et al. ("malicious users"), Errico et al. ("agents who become unintentional adversaries by over-stepping their role"), and Krakauer et al. (confused deputy as a server-tier ceiling). Only Errico et al. proposes per-user/role behavioural baselines, and it reports no implementation or evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- [Both] **Hou, Zhao, Wang, Wang, "Model Context Protocol (MCP): Landscape, Security Threats, and Future Research Directions"** (2025-03-30). It defines a 4-phase server lifecycle (creation, deployment, operation, maintenance) with 16 activities. Its threat taxonomy has **four attacker types: malicious developers, external attackers, malicious users, and security flaws**, covering 16 threat scenarios, with safeguards per lifecycle phase. It is the canonical early taxonomy and one of the few that lists the user/caller as an attacker. — [arXiv:2503.23278](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23278)
- [Both, strongest A→S content] **Errico, Ngiam, Sojan, "Securing the Model Context Protocol (MCP): Risks, Controls, and Governance"** (2025-11-25). It names three adversaries: content-injection attackers, supply-chain attackers, and "agents who become unintentional adversaries by over-stepping their role". §II-B3 says such an agent "may ... use high-privilege tools when lower-privilege alternatives exist" and "persist in attempting blocked operations through alternative paths". The proposed controls are per-user authentication with scoped authorization, provenance tracking, containerized sandboxing, inline policy enforcement with DLP and anomaly detection, and private registries or gateways. §III-D3 proposes "behavioral baselines ... typical tools used per user and role, typical tool call sequences", with alerts on "unusual data volume spikes, activity during after hours". The paper reports **no implementation or evaluation** of the baselines (I read the HTML up to character 100,000 of 109,301). — [arXiv:2511.20920](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.20920); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20920)
- [Mostly S→A] **Gaire et al., "Systematization of Knowledge: Security and Safety in the Model Context Protocol Ecosystem"** (2025-12-09). Its taxonomy separates adversarial security threats (indirect prompt injection, tool poisoning) from epistemic safety hazards (alignment failures in distributed tool delegation). It analyses the Resources/Prompts/Tools primitives and surveys defences "from cryptographic provenance (ETDI) to runtime intent verification". — [arXiv:2512.08290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08290)
- [Both] **Shen, Toyoda, Leung, "MCP-38: A Comprehensive Threat Taxonomy for Model Context Protocol Systems (v1.0)"** (2026-03-18). It defines 38 threat categories, built by protocol decomposition, multi-framework cross-mapping, incident synthesis and remediation-surface categorization. Each category maps to STRIDE, OWASP Top 10 for LLM Apps 2025, and OWASP's agentic Top 10. — [arXiv:2603.18063](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18063)
- [Mostly S→A] **Rostamzadeh et al., "MCP-DPT: A Defense-Placement Taxonomy and Coverage Analysis for MCP Security"** (2026-04-08). It organises attacks by the component responsible for enforcement across **six MCP layers**, under "adversaries controlling tools, servers, or ecosystem components". Mapping existing defences shows "uneven and predominantly tool-centric protection", with gaps at the host-orchestration, transport and supply-chain layers. — [arXiv:2604.07551](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07551)
- [Both] **Acharya, Gupta, "A Formal Security Framework for MCP-Based AI Agents" (MCPSHIELD)** (2026-04-07). It proposes 7 threat categories and 23 attack vectors across 4 surfaces, grounded in >177,000 MCP tools, plus a labelled-transition-system verification model. It compares 12 defences: "no existing single defense covers more than 34 percent", against 91% theoretical coverage for its reference architecture (capability-based access control, tool attestation, information-flow tracking, runtime policy). The name collides with MCPShield (arXiv:2602.14281), a different paper. — [arXiv:2604.05969](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05969)
- [Mostly S→A] **Anbiaee et al., "Security Threat Modeling for Emerging AI-Agent Protocols: A Comparative Analysis of MCP, A2A, Agora, and ANP"** (2026-02-11). Published in *J. Inf. Sec. Appl.* 2026, art. 104645, doi:10.1016/j.jisa.2026.104645. It identifies 12 protocol-level risks, rated qualitatively (likelihood × impact) per phase. Its MCP case study quantifies "wrong-provider tool execution under multi-server composition". — [arXiv:2602.11327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11327)
- [Both, server-developer view] **Krakauer et al., "Where Does MCP Security End? A Three-Tier Mitigation Mapping Framework for the OWASP MCP Top 10"** (2026-11-02). It maps the OWASP MCP Top 10 to server-primary, gateway-primary and protocol-primary tiers using a decision tree, built with Design Science Research. It was validated on an enterprise Managed-File-Transfer MCP server in Go. Its main result is an "architectural ceiling": confused-deputy and prompt-injection weaknesses "cannot be completely mitigated" in a server's SDLC. — [arXiv:2611.02611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02611)
- [Both] **Narajala, Habler, "Enterprise-Grade Security for the Model Context Protocol (MCP): Frameworks and Mitigation Strategies"** (2025-04-11). It offers enterprise mitigation frameworks and implementation strategies derived from threat modelling of MCP implementations. — [arXiv:2504.08623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08623)
- [S→A] **Fang et al., "We Should Identify and Mitigate Third-Party Safety Risks in MCP-Powered Agent Systems"** (position, 2025-06-16). It argues that third-party MCP service providers, outside the LLM developer's control, are a new safety risk. — [arXiv:2506.13666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13666)
- [S→A] **Maloyan et al., "Breaking the Protocol: Security Analysis of the MCP Specification..."** (2026-01-24). It finds three protocol-level flaws: no capability attestation, sampling without origin authentication, and implicit trust propagation across servers. In 847 scenarios on 5 servers, MCP raises attack success by 23–41% over non-MCP integrations. Its MCPSec extension cuts ASR from 52.8% to 12.4% at 8.3 ms per message. — [arXiv:2601.17549](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17549)
- [S→A] **Huang et al., "MCP Threat Modeling and Analyzing Vulnerabilities to Prompt Injection with Tool Poisoning"** (2026-03-23). It applies STRIDE and DREAD to five components (host/client, LLM, server, ...) and focuses on client-side weaknesses. — [arXiv:2603.22489](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22489)
- [Both] **Wang et al., "MCPGuard: Automatically Detecting Vulnerabilities in MCP Servers"** (2025-10-27). Despite the title, it is a survey with three threat categories: agent hijacking from protocol design, traditional web vulnerabilities in MCP servers, and supply chain. It reviews server-side scanning, agentic auditing, zero-trust registries and runtime monitoring. The name collides with MCP-Guard (arXiv:2508.10991). — [arXiv:2510.23673](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.23673)
- [Both] **Karanjai et al., "When Agents Act on Web3: An Attack-Surface Survey of MCP, Skills, and Tool Calling"** (2026-08-18). The share of deployed MCP tools that modify external state rose from **27% to 65%** of tool use. Four blockchain properties (irreversibility, signing authority, continuous autonomy, sequence-level composition) change the threat model. — [arXiv:2608.17275](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17275)
- [Both] **Bucknall et al., "From TCP/IP to MCP: Security and Governance Lessons from Internet Protocols for Agentic AI"** (2026-10-19). It is a governance analysis arguing that agent protocols must prioritise security early. — [arXiv:2610.26624](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26624)
- [A→S, review] **Surapani et al., "Authorization Architectures for Tool-Using AI Agents"** (2026-09-14, 70 pp.). It names MCP explicitly. Its principal hierarchy runs human user → operator/deployer → orchestrator agent → sub-agent → tool endpoint, across five layers: identity and credential lifecycle, delegation and scope propagation, just-in-time enforcement at PEPs, prompt injection as an authorization bypass, and more. Its requirement is that every action be "traceable to a human principal, bounded by what that human actually delegated, and contestable". — [arXiv:2609.15906](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15906)
- [unknown direction; metadata only] **Gautam, Tiwary, "A Survey on Security of the Model Context Protocol: Documented Incidents, Defense Frameworks, and Coverage Gaps in Agentic AI"**, IEEE ICoSICS 2026 (2026-09-01). I saw only the title and metadata in the Exa index. — [doi:10.1109/icosics70095.2026.11746554](https://doi.org/10.1109/icosics70095.2026.11746554)

### Inferences
- The dominant taxonomy axis is *where the malicious input enters*: tool metadata, tool output, server code, registry, transport. The caller is a first-class adversary only in Hou et al. (malicious users) and Errico et al. (over-stepping agents). A taxonomy organised around "legitimate agent misusing legitimate tools" (excessive agency, over-broad reads, cross-tool exfiltration through allowed tools) is still largely missing. Our framework can claim that gap, but should cite Errico §II-B3/§III-D3 as the closest prior statement.
- Krakauer et al.'s "architectural ceiling" supports the gateway/runtime layer: some confused-deputy risk cannot be fixed inside the server's code.

### Gaps
- The IEEE ICoSICS survey was not opened beyond metadata; its taxonomy and direction are unknown.
- MCP-DPT's six layer names and Narajala & Habler's control list were not read past the abstract.

## Which benchmarks and datasets exist for MCP security, what do they contain, what direction do they cover, and can they evaluate a server-side risk scorer?

### Takeaway
I verified about 19 MCP security benchmarks or evaluation methods (Jun 2025 – Nov 2026). The large ones (MCPTox, MSB, MCP-SafetyBench, MCP-AttackBench, COPEX, MCP-TDP, the cross-framework study) are **S→A**: poisoned metadata or outputs attacking the agent. None carries a stable caller identity with multi-day history. Only a few test the agent-as-threat direction: MCPHunt (credential propagation across servers by a non-malicious agent), FragBench (malicious goals split across sessions, linked per user), RAC's TraceBench (authorization drift), HCP (principal binding, scoped capability), and the Prompts-Don't-Protect / zero-trust-SDK experiments (unauthorized tool invocation). These can test pieces of a server-side scorer, not a per-agent behaviour baseline.

### Cited Findings
- [Both] **MCPSecBench** (Yang et al., 2025-08-17; Lingnan Univ. tech report). It formalises a "secure MCP" and defines 17 attack types across 4 attack surfaces (extending taxonomies with protocol- and host-side threats). It tests Claude, OpenAI and Cursor hosts: every surface yields compromises, and existing protections succeed <30% on average. — [arXiv:2508.13220](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13220)
- [S→A] **MCPTox** (Wang et al., 2025-08-19; AAAI, doi:10.1609/aaai.v40i42.40895). Tool poisoning via metadata on real servers, with no execution needed: 45 servers, 353 tools, 1,348 cases (repo note). — [arXiv:2508.14925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14925)
- [S→A] **MSB, MCP Security Bench** (Zhang et al., 2025-10-14; ICLR 2026). 12 attacks (name collision, preference manipulation, description injection, out-of-scope parameter requests, user-impersonating responses, false-error escalation, tool transfer, retrieval injection, mixed), run on real tools via MCP. 9 agents, 10 domains, 405 tools, 2,000 instances, scored with the Net Resilient Performance metric. "Models with stronger performance are more vulnerable." — [arXiv:2510.15994](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15994)
- [Mostly S→A] **MCP-SafetyBench** (Zong et al., 2025-12-17). Real servers in 5 domains (browser automation, financial analysis, location navigation, repository management, web search). 20 attack types across server, host and *user* sides, in multi-turn, cross-server tasks; every model tested is vulnerable, with a safety–utility trade-off. — [arXiv:2512.15163](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15163)
- [S→A] **MCP-Guard / MCP-AttackBench** (Xing et al., 2025-08-14). A three-stage defence: static scan, fine-tuned E5 detector (96.01% accuracy), LLM arbitrator. It ships MCP-AttackBench, 70,000+ samples (70,448, repo note), GPT-4-augmented. — [arXiv:2508.10991](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10991)
- [Both] **MCIP** (Jing et al., 2025-05-20; EMNLP 2025). A MAESTRO-guided analysis of MCP's missing safety mechanisms, a refined "Model Contextual Integrity Protocol", and a fine-grained taxonomy of unsafe MCP behaviours with benchmark and training data. — [arXiv:2505.14590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14590)
- [A→S] **MCPHunt** (Li et al., 2026-04-30; NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations & Datasets). The **non-adversarial** case: faithful tool composition turns benign read/write permissions into credential propagation across server boundaries. Canary-based taint tracking over 3,615 traces, 5 models, 147 tasks and 9 mechanism families. Policy-violating propagation runs **11.5–41.3%** in every model and is concentrated in browser-mediated flows; prompt mitigation brings it as low as 0.3%. v2 corrects v1's simulated taint-guard and agreement claims. Data on Hugging Face. — [arXiv:2604.27819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27819)
- [A→S] **FragBench** (Mehta et al., 2026-05-10). Malicious goals from 24 real cyber-incident campaigns are split into benign-looking fragments across separate sessions. It includes per-fragment judge verdicts, sandboxed traces (24 FastMCP servers, repo note) and matched benign cover sessions. — [arXiv:2605.11029](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11029)
- [Mostly S→A] **COPEX** (Birhan et al., 2026-10-03; NeurIPS 2026 Agents-in-the-Wild workshop). It isolates the model as MCP client: 25 attack types, 125 scenarios, 4 entry surfaces (model/agent, client, server/tool, transport). Over 9 models and 3,375 trials, mean ASR is 64.4%; input-plus-context scanning cuts it by 49.6%. — [arXiv:2610.04378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04378)
- [S→A] **MCP-TDP, "When the Manual Lies"** (Liu et al., 2026-05-22). Tool-description poisoning: 32 cases in 6 risk categories. GPT-4o reaches ~100% ASR in six high-risk scenarios. Prompt guardrails can backfire (their "Firewall Fallacy"). They propose "Reactive Self-Correction". — [arXiv:2605.24069](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24069)
- [S→A] **TrustShiftProbe** (Rostamzadeh et al., 2026-08-24). Servers behave well, then defect after an interaction threshold. Its defence, SHIELD, audits payloads against baselines learned in clean trust windows: ASR falls from 69.5% to 42.7% (repo note). This is the only temporal-baseline MCP work found, but it baselines **servers**, not callers. — [arXiv:2608.23763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23763)
- [Both] **MCP Pitfall Lab** (Hao et al., 2026-04-23). Developer pitfalls as reproducible scenarios across metadata, untrusted outputs, cross-tool flows, multimodal inputs and privileged sinks. Outcomes are validated from MCP traces, not agent self-report. — [arXiv:2604.21477](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21477)
- [Both, several A→S invariants] **HCP, "From Tool Connection to Execution Control: Benchmarking Security Invariants in MCP-Style Agent Runtimes"** (T. Liu, 2026-06-27). Eight invariants, including **principal binding, scoped capability invocation, source-and-target data-flow authorization, deny-path audit**. On 10 cases: a naive runtime permits all, a mitigation baseline permits 6, HCP blocks 10. — [arXiv:2606.29073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29073)
- [Mostly S→A] **GatewayBench** (Zheng et al., 2026-10-16). The first MCP-gateway benchmark: 524 tasks, 6 domains, 11,118 real tools from 251 servers, 7 deployed gateways, flattened vs hierarchical designs, benign and adversarial tasks. Gateway design strongly affects completion, efficiency and attack resilience. — [arXiv:2610.23449](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23449)
- [S→A] **"MCP Tool Poisoning in Agentic AI Frameworks: A Cross-Framework Measurement Study"** (Shaikh et al., 2026-11-02; AISec '26 @ CCS). A 25-class root-cause taxonomy and the open `mcpvenom` tool: 9,720 cells (12 frameworks × 18 models × 45 scenarios) with real execution. ASR is 22.9%, and 12.1% of vulnerable cells verifiably exfiltrate a secret. No framework implements intentional defence; a regex scanner rejects 20/45 scenarios at 7.2% FPR; all 5 disclosures were rejected. — [arXiv:2611.03555](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03555)
- [Both, partly MCP] **RAS-Eval** (Fu et al., 2025-06-18). 80 test cases and 3,802 attack tasks across 11 CWE categories, with tools in JSON, LangGraph and MCP formats. — [arXiv:2506.15253](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15253)
- [A→S] **Mahapatra, "Public-Sharing Labels and Verbatim Field Egress in an MCP-to-A2A Agent Configuration"** (2026-09-01). A controlled 3-arm study (CONFIDENTIAL header / none / "PUBLIC – OK TO SHARE") over 10 record scenarios with deterministic scoring. It measures whether labels change verbatim egress of record fields across a local MCP→A2A path. — [arXiv:2609.01693](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01693)
- [Method] **Ahmed, Abbas, "Labels Are Not Endpoints: Treatment Leakage and Construct Validity in MCP Agent Security Evaluation"** (2026-08-13). An audit of 10,200 execution rows found that treatment metadata gated the ATTACK_SUCCESS label; 58 historical attack labels were corrected to authorized benign completions. — [arXiv:2608.12880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12880)
- [Method, S→A] **Abasıkeleş Turgut et al., record-level audit of CASCADE (MCP prompt-injection defence)** (2026-04-18). On a frozen 5,000-sample corpus, the headline 11.70% FPR depends on how allow/review/block collapse to a binary label; decision conventions make published MCP-defence figures incomparable. — [arXiv:2604.17125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17125)

### Inferences
- **Usability for a server-side scorer.** S→A benchmarks (MCPTox, MSB, MCP-AttackBench, MCP-TDP, COPEX) test whether the *agent* resists poisoned servers. They have no "server under attack by its caller" label and no caller identity, so they cannot evaluate per-agent baselining. The closest usable pieces:
  - MCPHunt: cross-server credential flow by a benign agent; checks an "unsafe sequence / cross-boundary" signal.
  - FragBench: per-user linkage across sessions; the only one with cross-session caller grouping found.
  - RAC TraceBench: authorization drift within a session; see the defences section.
  - HCP's 10 invariant cases.
  - MCP-SafetyBench's user-side attacks.
- Two method papers (Labels Are Not Endpoints; the CASCADE audit) and Chen et al.'s scanner study in the measurement section all show that MCP security numbers are fragile to labelling and decision conventions. Our evaluation should state its labelling rule and never derive labels from the scorer.

### Gaps
- No benchmark found carries per-agent, multi-day histories or labelled "ordinary vs gray" agent behaviour against a protected server. This agrees with the repo's earlier finding; this pass found no counterexample in the abstracts read.
- CrossMCP-Bench (Hugging Face, an A→S authorization-conditioned benchmark) and the MDPI "Runtime Policy Enforcement for MCP-Based LLM Agents" (*Electronics* 15(13):2829) appear in the repo note but were **not re-verified in this pass**.

## Which empirical studies measured the MCP server ecosystem (vulnerabilities, permissions, credential handling, registries)?

### Takeaway
25 measurement or ecosystem-audit papers are listed here (Jun 2025 – Oct 2026), scaling from ~1.9K servers (Hasan et al.) to 64K–129K (MCPZoo). Repeated findings:
- Many internet-facing servers have no authentication: 40.55% of 7,973 remote servers, and 91.8% of 414 audited servers lack OAuth.
- Servers hold broad host privileges: network/system APIs in >1,200 servers; 16.4% of triaged servers have filesystem capabilities beyond their declared function.
- Taint-style handler bugs exist.
- Registries drift silently.
- Automated scanners are unreliable: <50% of sampled alerts are true positives.

Several measurements are **A→S-relevant**: an attacker-steered argument, or any unauthenticated caller, reaches privileged server operations.

### Cited Findings
- [Both] **Hasan et al., "MCP at First Glance"** (2025-06-16; ACM TOSEM 2026, doi:10.1145/3814959). 1,899 open-source servers, 8 vulnerability types (3 overlap traditional ones). 7.2% have general vulnerabilities, 5.5% have MCP-specific tool poisoning, 66% have code smells. — [arXiv:2506.13538](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13538); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3814959)
- [S→A, host protection] **Li et al., "We Urgently Need Privilege Management in MCP"** (2025-07-05; IEEE MASS per repo `refs.bib`, venue not re-verified). Static analysis of 2,562 MCP apps in 23 categories: network APIs appear in 1,438 servers, system-resource APIs in 1,237. "Less popular plugins often contain disproportionately high-risk operations." Case studies show privilege escalation and data tampering; the paper calls for dynamic permission models. — [arXiv:2507.06250](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06250)
- [S→A] **Li, Gao, "A First Look at the Security Issues in the MCP Ecosystem"** (2025-10-18; DSN 2026). 67,057 servers across 6 registries. Weak vetting and ownership checks enable server hijacking, and hosts execute LLM-selected tools "without independent verification". MCPInspect flags 833 vulnerable servers and 18 with suspicious descriptions. — [arXiv:2510.16558](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16558)
- [Both] **Guo et al., "A Measurement Study of MCP Ecosystem"** (2025-09-29). MCPCrawler covered six markets for 14 days: 8,401 valid projects (8,060 servers, 341 clients). More than half of listings are invalid or low-value; it finds dependency monocultures. — [arXiv:2509.25292](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25292)
- [Both, dataset] **Lin et al., MCPCorpus** (2025-06-30). ~14K servers and 300 clients with 20+ attributes. — [arXiv:2506.23474](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.23474)
- [Both, dataset] **Wu et al., MCPZoo** (2025-12-17). 129,059 servers (56,053 distinct), of which 16,356 run. — [arXiv:2512.15144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15144)
- [Both] **Chen et al., "Rethinking MCP Security: A Large-Scale Study of Runtime MCP Servers and Security Scanner Reliability"** (2026-07-13). Extended MCPZoo: 64,611 unique servers, 37,288 dynamically analysable. Scanners call 96.89% of servers risky, but "less than 50% of sampled alerts are true positives", and scanners disagree. — [arXiv:2607.11086](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11086)
- [A→S] **Padilla, "Exposed by Design: A Dynamic Security Assessment of Internet-Facing MCP Servers at Scale"** (2026-07-31). Of 640 confirmed production servers, 414 were dynamically audited with Corvus (34 modules, 10 vulnerability classes), giving 68 reportable vulnerabilities (SQLi, SSRF to cloud metadata, path traversal). **91.8% lack OAuth**, 687 tool instances expose shell execution without access control, and 41.6% of servers vanish within 3 days. — [arXiv:2608.00150](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00150)
- [A→S] **Huang et al., "My Credentials, Please: Detecting Filesystem Capability Laundering in MCP Servers at Scale"** (2026-10-15). Client approval of a tool call grants the server process broad host filesystem rights, so **attacker-steered arguments** can read files outside the tool's declared contract. 21,097 servers from 9 registries: 787 of 4,806 triaged servers (16.4%) have filesystem capabilities despite non-filesystem classifications; 150 confirmed vulnerable; 13 advisories, including **two CVEs in Anthropic's Git MCP server**. — [arXiv:2610.22301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22301)
- [A→S] **Sun et al., VIPER-MCP** (2026-05-20). Detects and exploits taint-style vulnerabilities: a natural-language input reaches shell, network or filesystem sinks in tool handlers (RCE). — [arXiv:2605.21392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21392)
- [A→S] **Shi et al., "Mitigating Taint-Style Vulnerabilities in MCP Servers via Security-Aware Tool Descriptions"** (2026-07-08). It studies vulnerable code patterns and community responses, and mitigates via descriptions. — [arXiv:2607.07461](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07461)
- [Both] **Yan et al., MCPPrivacyDetector, "What Happens Locally, Leaks Globally"** (2026-06-19). Credentials, API keys and PII "cross the local/LLM boundary simply by being returned, logged, or raised inside a tool handler"; cross-language static analysis. — [arXiv:2606.21338](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21338)
- [Both] **Yan et al., MICRYSCOPE** (2025-12-03). Cryptographic misuse in MCP implementations; MCP offers no authenticity or confidentiality guarantees. — [arXiv:2512.03775](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.03775)
- [Both] **Kraishan, "Same Name, Different Server: A Security Census of Silent Drift"** (2026-09-12). 21,643 registry servers and 72,606 versions (Aug 2026). Unauthenticated network exposure: 9.57%. 51.1% of multi-version servers changed what they advertise, 40.6% silently, and 4.2% redirected their remote endpoint. Drift goes with ~3× odds of a high-severity finding (OR 2.96). — [arXiv:2609.14119](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14119)
- [Both] **Bharti, "Registry Descriptions Go Stale Unevenly"** (2026-08-02). 120 snapshots of the official registry over 88.6 days (3,510 → 18,966 servers); description-level audits go stale. v2 corrects five claims. — [arXiv:2608.00997](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00997)
- [Both, method] **Afsar, "What a Random Draw from the MCP Registry Contains"** (2026-09-10). From a 24,135-server census, a random 400 npm/stdio servers: only 48.8% complete `initialize` (vs 66.7% in a curated frame), and 37.5% never start. Ecosystem studies select for working servers. — [arXiv:2609.10962](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10962)

### Inferences
- For a server-side scorer, the most useful measured facts are three. Capability laundering (16.4% of servers whose capabilities exceed their declared function) says static tool scoring should price the *actual* capability, not the declared one. The taint-style handlers (VIPER-MCP) say argument-level risk matters. Silent drift (40.6%) says static scores must be re-run when a server's tool list changes.
- The figures disagree on how many servers lack authentication (40.55%, 91.8%, 9.57%, "all of ~2,000", 95% OAuth on PaaS). The samples differ: live remote servers, a dynamic-audit subset, a registry pattern scan, a PaaS-hosted subset. Report the sample with any figure.

### Gaps
- No measurement study observes real *caller* behaviour against production servers (which agents call what, how often, with what breadth). The honeypot evidence in the repo's earlier note (GreyNoise; workspace-tools-mcp) is non-academic.

## Which defences exist, and which protect servers from agents versus agents from servers?

### Takeaway
I verified 46 MCP defence papers (Apr 2025 – Nov 2026). Twenty are labelled A→S: they defend the server or the organisation's data from the agent's calls. The A→S subsection holds 25 entries, 5 of which are labelled Both: gateways and proxies with ABAC/RBAC, capability tokens, authorization-drift checks, tenant isolation, secret brokers, signed mandates, RBAC over SQL, and post-training for least privilege. The rest, mostly detectors, signing and attestation, sandboxing of server code, and provenance binding, protect the agent/host from servers (S→A) or both. Among academic A→S defences, enforcement is rule- or capability-based per call or per session. None found grades a call against the *same agent's own multi-week history*. Errico et al. propose that only as an unevaluated control; MCP Guardian and Governed MCP use fixed per-token rate limits.

### Inferences
- **A→S defences share a pattern:** a deterministic enforcement point outside the model, at the gateway, proxy, wrapper or server, using capability tokens or policy. Three results motivate it: Prompts Don't Protect (prompt allowlists never reach zero), the zero-trust SDK study (in-body checks leak 21.1%), and the Stochastic Deputy (validated tenant parameters still serve 26/26). Our gateway sits in the same place. Our addition is a graded per-call risk that combines static tool risk with a per-agent behaviour baseline, as opposed to allow/deny rules.
- **The temporal/behavioural dimension is thin.** RAC tracks lineage within a session, ChainWatch uses a session HMM, AegisMCP uses global graph novelty, MCP Guardian and Governed MCP use fixed rate limits, and TrustShiftProbe baselines servers. Per-caller longitudinal baselining appears only as Errico et al.'s unevaluated proposal.
- **Least privilege** has three forms here: structural (registry filtering at discovery, removing scope parameters from schemas), capability-token based (AgentCap, AIP, KedgeFlow, ACLE-MCP), and learned (task-conditioned post-training). Static scoring of "what this tool could do" complements all three.

- Neither InfrastructureSentinel (AAAI) nor VISTA was opened past the index summary, so their evaluation numbers are not recorded.
- Mandato's and AIRGuard's evaluation numbers were not read (abstracts truncated at 650 characters).

## Which works discuss agent identity, authentication and per-caller attribution in MCP (OAuth 2.1, delegation, non-human identity)?

### Takeaway
Nine MCP-specific works address caller identity. Two are cross-listed from other sections. The empirical picture is consistent: many remote MCP servers authenticate nobody, OAuth deployments are flawed (dynamic client registration flaws in 96.6% of tested OAuth servers), and SDKs struggle with human and service-account callers on the same server. Proposed fixes:
- a production enterprise gateway with RFC 8693 token exchange;
- Invocation-Bound Capability Tokens (AIP);
- SDK extensions for per-tool authorization;
- an authorization broker that confines a compromised sub-agent to its delegated task.

General agent-identity proposals (OIDC-A, Agentic JWT, authenticated delegation, DID/VC identity) do not name MCP in their abstracts.

### Cited Findings
- [A→S] **Zhou et al., "A First Measurement Study on Authentication Security in Real-World Remote MCP Servers"** (2026-05-21). 7,973 live remote servers, of which **40.55% expose tools without authentication**. - **Specification context (repo note, spec pages not re-opened here):** MCP authorization is OPTIONAL, and stdio implementations SHOULD NOT use it. Revision 2026-07-28 removes the `initialize` handshake and protocol-level sessions. — [MCP spec 2025-06-18, Authorization](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/authorization); [2026-07-28 changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/changelog)
- **Not MCP-specific (checked, MCP not named in the abstract; not counted):** OIDC-A 1.0 ([arXiv:2509.25974](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25974)); Agentic JWT ([arXiv:2509.13597](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13597)); South et al., "Authenticated Delegation and Authorized AI Agents" ([arXiv:2501.09674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09674)); Huang et al., zero-trust DID/VC identity ([arXiv:2505.19301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19301)).

### Inferences
- Per-caller attribution at an MCP server is not guaranteed by the protocol. Authorization is optional, absent on stdio, often missing or flawed on remote servers. A per-agent baseline therefore needs a gateway that establishes identity (OAuth subject, service account, or token-exchange subject). This is exactly the architecture reported in production by Kumar et al. and argued by Li et al. and AIP. Our framework should state this dependency and cite these works as the identity layer it builds on.
- The user/non-user persona split (Kumar et al.; Li et al.) matters for baselining: human-driven and service-account agents have different normal rhythms. A per-agent baseline keyed on the token subject handles both if the gateway normalises credentials.

### Gaps
- No MCP work found measures how often real deployments share one credential across many agents ("the agent id may stand for the whole org"). This is the attribution failure that matters most for per-agent baselines.
- The caller-identity-confusion study (the only direct measurement of server-side caller ambiguity) is withdrawn; this remains an open empirical question.

## What is the threat direction of every work, and what is the special A→S picture? (includes attack papers, borderline items and the scoring hand-off)

### Takeaway
This note counts **132 unique MCP-specific works**: 129 arXiv papers plus 3 DOI-only. By primary label: **34 A→S** (1 "mostly"), **50 S→A** (7 "mostly"), **47 Both or neutral** (2 of them evaluation-method papers), and 1 unknown. A→S work is recent (mostly Mar–Nov 2026) and concentrated in authorization/gateway defences and a few measurements (unauthenticated servers, capability laundering, taint-style handlers). The attack literature is overwhelmingly S→A. Its A→S-relevant part is that malicious content **assembles legitimate tools into exfiltration chains** (Parasites, Trivial Trojans, Log-To-Leak, MCPHunt's non-adversarial version). That is the same data movement a server-side scorer sees as an over-broad or unusual sequence from the agent.

### Inferences
- For the paper's related-work framing:
  - A→S is a real but young strand. Cite it as authorization and identity infrastructure our scorer runs on (gateway, OAuth subject, capability tokens).
